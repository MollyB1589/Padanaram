# Padanaram Village — Researched History

Source notes compiled for the Padanaram Business Association website
(`padanaramsouthdartmouth.com`). Everything below is drawn from the sources
listed at the bottom; where sources disagree, the disagreement is noted rather
than smoothed over.

Padanaram is a coastal village in South Dartmouth, Massachusetts, at the mouth
of the Apponagansett River on Buzzards Bay. Locally: *paid-nair-um*.

---

## The four names

| Name | Period | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Apponagansett | Wampanoag | Commonly given as "waiting place at the ledge" |
| Ponagansett / Ponaganset | 17th–18th c. | English rendering of the Wampanoag name |
| Akin's Wharf (also "Akin's Landing") | c. 1778–1798 | For Elihu Akin, ~20 years after the Revolution |
| Padanaram | c. 1805– | Named by Laban Thacher for the biblical Paddan Aram |

Laban Thacher came from Harwich, on Cape Cod, around 1805. He is said to have
seen an analogy between his own life and that of the biblical Laban, who dwelt
in Paddan Aram — "the field of Aram" in Aramaic — a plain in northwest
Mesopotamia along the Euphrates. The Wampanoag-derived name was dropped.

---

## Chronology

### Before 1652 — Apponagansett
The Wampanoag lived on this coast for a thousand years and more before European
colonization. The Apponagansett, Acushnet and Acoaxet bands gave their names to
Dartmouth, New Bedford and Westport respectively.

### 1652 — The Dartmouth Purchase
On **March 7, 1652**, Plymouth Colony "Purchasers" and "Old Comers" — John
Winslow, William Bradford, Myles Standish, Thomas Southworth and John Cooke —
acquired roughly **115,000 acres** from the Wampanoag, represented by the sachem
Ousamequin (Massasoit) and his son Wamsutta. The tract, "Old Dartmouth," became
present-day Dartmouth, New Bedford, Fairhaven, Acushnet and Westport.

Recorded consideration: *"…thirty yards of cloth, eight moose skins, fifteen
axes, fifteen hoes, fifteen pairs of breeches, eight blankets, two kettles, one
clock, two English Pounds in Wampum, eight pair of shoes, one iron pot, and ten
shillings…"*

This 1652 date is the "Est. 1652" on the village logo.

### 1675–76 — King Philip's War
Apponagansett was the part of Dartmouth that suffered most. On the east bank of
the river, a short distance north of the bridge, stood **John Russell's garrison
house** at Russell's Orchard — a dwelling ringed by a wooden stockade where the
neighborhood took refuge. In **July 1675** a group of Native people surrendered
their weapons and sought refuge there; Russell offered them Little Island. The
site was excavated in **1951** and is a National Historic Landmark.

### 1699 / 1787–1791 — The Quakers
Friends had used the Apponagansett site since at least **1699**, when the first
meeting house was built. In **1787** the Quakers of Old Dartmouth voted to
replace it; the old building came down in **1790** and the present
**Apponegansett Meeting House** was raised in **1791**. Records indicate ~2,000
Friends attended the opening. It is the oldest Quaker meeting house in
southeastern Massachusetts and among the best preserved; NRHP-listed **1991**.

### Mid-18th century — Shipbuilding
The river mouth "did not attract shipbuilding, whaling and salt works until the
mid-18th century," when it became a shipbuilding center. Brothers **James and
Elihu Akin** owned a shipyard, a tavern, large houses and other businesses on
the Apponagansett.

### September 1778 — Grey's Raid
Elihu Akin drove three Loyalists out of the village. In reprisal, British
raiding parties under **Major General Charles Grey** — sweeping the coast in
what is remembered as Grey's Raid, and burning New Bedford — sent a force to
Padanaram (then Akin's Landing) and **burned most of the village**, concentrating
on Akin's property. Akin was left with one small house on Potters Hill (the
**Elihu Akin House**, still standing). He never recovered financially and died
poor. Repairing the town's damage was reckoned at **£105,960** in 1778 — roughly
nine million dollars today.

For about twenty years afterward the village was called **Akin's Wharf** in his
honor.

### c. 1805 — Laban Thacher
Thacher and his family built a wharf, a shipyard, a windmill, a magnesia
factory, a saltworks, a school and a church. The saltworks — elaborate and
expensive — failed, and the village christened it **"Laban's Folly."** He gave
the village its present name.

### 19th century — Salt and whales
Padanaram became a **minor whaling port** and home to a **large saltworks**,
operating largely in support of New Bedford's whaling boom. **South Wharf has
been in operation since 1832.** As these industries died out the village became
largely residential, with yachting businesses, galleries, eateries and shops.

### 1828–1836 — The first bridge
**1828**: the **Dartmouth Bridge Company** was formed by Dartmouth residents to
bridge the Apponagansett. Construction began **February 7, 1834**; the bridge was
finished and opened in **1836**.

**1920**: Town Meeting approved **$22,500** for a wooden draw bridge and **$2,000**
to repair the center pier.

### 1877–1901 — The New Bedford Yacht Club
Formed **1877** with headquarters on Fish Island; incorporated by the
Commonwealth **January 25, 1878** "for the purpose of encouraging athletic
exercises, yachting and especially by maintaining a Club house." Moved to Pope
Island **1879**. Built its **Padanaram station in 1901** (clubhouse built 1901),
which became the club's main station after Pope Island was sold in **1941**. One
of the oldest yacht clubs in America.

> **Note on a common discrepancy:** some local accounts give 1906 for the
> Padanaram station. The club's own history and multiple secondary sources give
> **1901**. Worth confirming with the club before print.

### 1926–1966 — Concordia
- **1926** — Llewellyn Howland founds the Concordia Company.
- **1932** — transferred to his son **Waldo Howland** and Marblehead racing
  designer **C. Raymond Hunt**.
- **1938** — Hunt designs the **Concordia yawl**, with input from Llewellyn and
  Waldo Howland, Clinton Crane, Fenwick Williams and Frank Paine — a boat meant
  to cruise and race and to handle what Buzzards Bay does on a bad afternoon.
- **1938–1966** — **103 Concordias built**, the largest class of large
  one-design wooden sailboats.
- **1969** — Waldo Howland sells the business to Bill Pinney; the Howland family
  splits business from the South Wharf property.
- **1981** — Brodie MacGregor buys the company; **1987** — buys 14 acres at 300
  Gulf Road.
- **2006–2007** — South Wharf property goes to market; South Wharf takes over
  the former Concordia yard in 2007 and Concordia moves operations inland to
  Gulf Road as of **August 1, 2007**. Concordia had leased South Wharf for
  roughly **75 years**.

### 1936 — The present swing bridge
Built **1936**, under the supervision of **Robert A. Watkins**, with **Benjamin
Watkins** as general contractor. A swing-span on Gulf Road over the
Apponagansett River; vertical clearance closed is 9 ft at mean high water. It
still opens on the hour in season.

### The storms
- **September 21, 1938** — the Great New England Hurricane: gusts reported to
  183 mph, storm surge over 12 feet. Two-thirds of the boats in New Bedford
  Harbor were lost; at Concordia, moored boats were hurled onto the beach and
  the bridge. The town spent **$2,700** on bridge and causeway repairs to reopen
  quickly.
- **August 31, 1954** — Hurricane Carol: nearly 12-foot surge; crews resurfaced
  the causeway afterward.
- **August 1991** — Hurricane Bob: direct hit; the bridge was inoperable and
  impassable for about a week.

### September 5, 1985 — The Historic District
The **Padanaram Village Historic District** was added to the National Register
of Historic Places (ref. **85002010**). Roughly bounded by Elm, Water, Middle,
High, Pleasant, Prospect, Hill, School, Fremont and Bridge Streets. Federal,
Late Victorian and Colonial Revival architecture. Areas of significance:
industry, commerce, exploration/settlement, architecture, religion, maritime
history, social history. Period of significance: **1750s–1949**.

### 2016–2018 — The causeway rebuilt
Reconstruction began **June 2016**; the bridge closed permanently to traffic in
**August 2016**. The causeway reopened **June 11, 2018**. Project cost roughly
**$10.5 million** (construction contract ~$8.27M); contractor **John Rocchio
Corporation**. Work covered roadway, drainage, environmental, structural and
lighting improvements along Gulf Road and Smith Neck Road.

### 2020 — The Buoy Tree
Begun in the first COVID winter, when no suitable tree could be found for a
lighting ceremony. **Molly Armando**, who had started a moms' group at St.
Peter's Episcopal Church, took the idea to the Padanaram Business Association;
the inspiration was the lobster-trap trees people pose in front of at the
holidays. More than **300 buoys** were purchased and sold at **$5 each** to cover
the cost, then hand-painted by the Dartmouth Council on Aging, art students,
preschool and elementary children, and local artists. Now an annual village
tradition.

### Today
Padanaram has been voted **Best Harbor in the U.S.** by USHarbors.com in
**2019, 2022 and 2026**.

## Still standing

- The swing bridge (1936)
- New Bedford Yacht Club (1901)
- South Wharf — working since 1832
- Captains' and merchants' houses on Elm and Water Streets
- Apponegansett Meeting House (1791), Russells Mills Road
- Elihu Akin House, Potters Hill — the one property Grey's raiders left him
- Russell Garrison site — National Historic Landmark

## Open questions worth confirming locally

1. **New Bedford Yacht Club, 1901 vs. 1906** for the Padanaram station.
2. **Elihu Akin's dates** and the exact sequence of the September 1778 raid —
   the Dartmouth Heritage Preservation Trust holds the best material.
3. Whether the PBA wants the **1652 purchase terms** quoted in full. They are
   historically important and also a plain record of what was paid; the page as
   written quotes them without editorializing.
4. **PBA founding year** — the About page says "the 1970s." A firm date would be
   better.
5. Names of Padanaram-built or Padanaram-homeported **whaling vessels**, which
   would make the 19th-century section concrete.
